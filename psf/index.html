Professional Service Fees: What Is the Merchant Paying For?

The debate over broker fees in alternative finance.

Christina Duncan
Program Director, Secure Funder™

Professional service fees, often called PSFs, are fees charged directly by brokers to businesses seeking financing. The practice has a poor reputation in parts of alternative finance, especially when the fee is charged before financing is secured or is nonrefundable if the merchant is not approved. Still, supporters argue there is real work involved in trying to place a deal.

Why Some Brokers Charge Them

Brokers may spend time reviewing financials, packaging files, contacting funders, collecting stipulations, negotiating terms, and trying to find an approval. The argument is simple: the work has value even if the transaction does not fund.

Some brokers therefore charge a separate fee for that service, either upfront or during the process. That position becomes easier to understand when the broker is doing meaningful advisory or placement work rather than simply forwarding an application.

Why Others Object

The opposing view is just as simple: that is what the commission is for. Most brokers are already paid when a transaction funds. Deals that do not close are generally viewed as part of the cost of doing business.

Critics are particularly concerned when fees are:

  • charged before an approval exists
  • nonrefundable
  • poorly disclosed
  • large relative to the amount of financing sought
  • charged in addition to substantial compensation from the funder

A $500 or $1,000 fee looks very different on a $100,000 transaction than it does on a $5,000 transaction.

The Funder Perspective

Many funders do not support additional broker fees. Their concern is practical.

If a business receives $20,000 and immediately pays $2,000 to a broker, that is $2,000 less available for payroll, inventory, marketing, emergencies, or the payments on the financing itself. From the funder’s perspective, a large fee can weaken the transaction before the first payment is even made.

Some funders therefore restrict or prohibit brokers from charging merchants separately.

Where the Debate Really Is

The issue is probably not whether a broker can ever provide a service worth paying for. The harder questions are:

  • Was the fee clearly disclosed?
  • What exactly is the merchant paying for?
  • When is the fee considered earned?
  • Is it refundable if financing is not secured?
  • Is the amount reasonable relative to the financing?

Those details matter.

My Final Thoughts

There is a reasonable argument that professional work has value, even when financing is not ultimately secured. There is also a reasonable argument that brokerage is a success-based business and that commissions compensate brokers for both the deals that fund and the deals that do not.

The practice becomes harder to defend when a merchant pays a significant fee, receives little clarity about what it covers, and walks away without the financing they were trying to obtain.

Whether a professional service fee is reasonable may depend less on the name of the fee and more on the disclosure, amount, timing, and actual service provided.
